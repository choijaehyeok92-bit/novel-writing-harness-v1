# External Actors Canon

## Frederick I Barbarossa

- Holy Roman Emperor; Alexios's western counterpart in the "Two Romes" arrangement.
- In this timeline he accepts coordinated passage/logistics, meets Alexios as a rival-brother emperor, survives the Anatolian/Saleph route, and participates in the successful crusade.
- Relationship with Alexios: real respect and battlefield partnership mixed with long-term geopolitical rivalry.
- Post-crusade: obtains/steals enough Roman gunpowder knowledge to end the assumption of permanent Byzantine monopoly and returns west convinced that the western empire must be consolidated if it is not to fall permanently behind Constantinople.
- Knowing his remaining life is limited, Frederick increasingly gives **Henry VI the practical execution of the reform program**, so that the heir acquires governing experience, administrative authority, and responsibility before Frederick's death. Frederick remains the source of overriding sacral-military legitimacy; Henry becomes the principal executor and therefore also the immediate target of much princely resentment.
- Frederick uses the exceptional prestige of a successful crusading emperor and recoverer of the Holy City to push an unusually ambitious western-centralization settlement. Opposition from princes, bishops, and territorial lords is real and substantial; Frederick suppresses some resistance through personal authority and coercive leverage while obtaining consent elsewhere through negotiated concessions.
- Centralizing measures include systematic recovery/review of **Reichsgut**, stronger annual revenue/property reporting from imperial estates and regalia, broader use of imperial ministeriales in castles, finance, justice, customs, and military administration, stricter treatment of office as delegated service rather than private hereditary possession, expanded Landfrieden, stronger imperial-city and lesser-noble counterweights to great princes, and tightly guarded imperial gunpowder workshops/technical cadres.
- **Settlement A — CONFIRMED ALT-HISTORY:** after the fall of Henry the Lion, Frederick entrenches a settlement preventing the great Saxon and Bavarian territorial complexes from being recombined under a single magnate in a way that could recreate a Henry-the-Lion-scale counterpower. This is an intentional alternate-history strengthening of imperial policy, achieved through Frederick's personal authority, feudal/dynastic management, pressure, and bargaining rather than through effortless administrative command.
- **Settlement B — CONFIRMED ALT-HISTORY:** Frederick secures an imperial-princely compact/recognized succession practice that, when the reigning emperor has a legitimate eldest son without a disqualifying defect, the princes are to elect that heir as King of the Romans. The electoral form is retained, but Hohenstaufen hereditary expectation is deliberately strengthened. Frederick wins this only after major resistance and concessions, including confirmation or enlargement of important hereditary/security guarantees sought by princes; the resulting bargain strengthens both the dynasty and the territorial princes and therefore contains a built-in contradiction.
- Frederick does **not** convert the empire into a Byzantine-style unitary state. His achievement is a much denser network of imperial estates, ministeriales, cities, records, peace jurisdiction, technical monopolies, and dynastic succession expectations held together above all by his extraordinary personal prestige.
- **Death timing — LOCKED:** Frederick dies in **spring 1194, several weeks before Constantinople's founding-day festival**, at Nuremberg.
- On his deathbed he instructs Henry to secure his Sicilian rights. Frederick sees Sicily as a separate dynastic resource that could reduce Hohenstaufen dependence on imperial princes and strengthen the western empire against Constantinople. His final dynastic formula is **"Rome to the Hohenstaufen."**
- Frederick is buried at **Speyer Cathedral beside Beatrice**. Alexios later sends a silver-plated perpetual lamp with a Greek/Latin dedication to "Frederick, Imperator of the West" and his Jerusalem comrade.
- Current status through chapter 110: **deceased**. His death removes the personal authority that had suppressed contradictions created by his reforms and transfers both the strengthened imperial machinery and its accumulated opposition to Henry VI.

## Henry VI

- Frederick Barbarossa's eldest surviving imperial heir and designated political successor; already formed by years of practical responsibility under his father rather than appearing as an inexperienced ruler only after succession.
- Before Frederick's death, Henry is the principal executor of much of the western centralization program. This gives him genuine administrative authority and competence but also causes many princes to associate the burdens and intrusions of reform directly with him.
- Inherits a stronger imperial apparatus and a stronger Hohenstaufen succession expectation, but also an unstable bargain: great princes have obtained hereditary/security concessions at the same time that imperial authority has expanded.
- His immediate strategic problem after Frederick's death is to prove that the system depended on the dynasty rather than on Frederick's personal charisma. This makes decisive external success, especially the Sicilian inheritance claim through Constance, unusually attractive.
- Relationship with Constantinople is not bound by Frederick and Alexios's personal battlefield fraternity. Henry is therefore positioned to redefine the Two Romes arrangement in a colder and more competitive direction.
- **Status at the end of chapter 110:** Henry is still **Romanorum Rex / King of the Romans**, not yet Imperator in this timeline. Konstantinos Mesopotamites opens the first formal Byzantine audience by deliberately greeting him with that exact title rather than transferring Frederick's imperial recognition automatically.
- Presentation by chapter 110: young, controlled, intellectually sharp, physically leaner and more delicate than his father, and politically dangerous; he receives the Byzantine mission in a public court framed by bishops, princes and royal officers.

## Frederick VI, Duke of Swabia — CONFIRMED ALT-HISTORY SURVIVOR

- Historical baseline diverges: Frederick VI survives the crusade-era death that removed him historically and returns as a **crusade hero** and Duke of Swabia.
- He is Henry VI's younger brother and, by chapter 110, displays **personal loyalty to Henry and no overt ambition for the throne**.
- His lack of ambition does not remove his objective political value. Major princes visibly treat him with unusual warmth after Barbarossa's funeral, making him an alternative Hohenstaufen focal point against Henry's centralizing line whether he desires that role or not.
- Named princes visibly associated with this signal in chapter 110 include **Adolf of Altena (Archbishop of Cologne), Hermann I of Thuringia, Henry I of Brabant, Henry III of Limburg, and Albert I of Meissen**.
- Alexios predicts that Henry will eventually confine Frederick rather than execute him, because killing him would create a martyr and opposition banner. **This is prediction only: Frederick has not yet been confined on-page by the end of chapter 110.**
- Alexios explicitly orders Mesopotamites **not** to cultivate Frederick as a Byzantine proxy at this stage; premature contact could expose Roman intent and provoke Henry into eliminating his brother.

## Enrico Dandolo

- Venetian statesman encountered early in negotiations; becomes Doge in **1192**.
- Core motivation: preserve Venetian autonomy and commercial leverage against an increasingly self-sufficient and financially central Roman Empire.
- By ch97 he understands that rising Roman trade volume can still be a strategic Venetian defeat if pricing, clearing, debt, standards, insurance and shipping gravity all move to Constantinople.
- Uses/encourages a large selloff of Roman state securities; Roman countermeasures blunt the attack.
- Opens toward Henry in the western empire: "Rome counters Rome."
- Current status: principal external economic/strategic antagonist in the consolidation era.

## Kilij Arslan II

- Sultan of Rum during Anatolian campaign.
- Divergence: avoids the same early fragmentation among sons because Roman strength/crusade threat changes incentives.
- Defeated after Philomelion/Iconium campaign; abdication/yield creates Roman-dependent succession.
- Current political role: displaced elder ruler; effective initiative passes to Kaykhusraw/client arrangement.

## Ghiyath al-Din Kaykhusraw

- Youngest son/successor in the altered Rum settlement.
- Installed/recognized under decisive Roman military-political leverage.
- Rules a reduced eastern Rum polity as a dependent/client ruler while major recovered Anatolian zones pass to direct Roman administration.
- Current function: buffer/client whose survival depends heavily on Roman backing.

## Saladin (Salah al-Din)

- Ayyubid ruler and principal Muslim opponent in the Jerusalem campaign.
- Defeated strategically but not destroyed; negotiates rather than fighting to annihilation.
- Agrees to Christian restoration of Jerusalem under protections for Muslim residents/worship, truce, prisoner and territorial/financial terms.
- Character framing: respected as a serious and often honorable adversary rather than demonized.
- Status: dies by 1193; succession fragments among sons/brother as expected.

## Conrad I of Montferrat

- Becomes king of restored Jerusalem after the successful coalition campaign.
- Connected to Byzantine court through Montferrat family/John (Reinier) ties.
- Rules a kingdom whose settlement protects Muslim internal legal/religious life while retaining explicit Christian monarchy and medieval confessional hierarchy.
- Guy de Lusignan's challenge/conspiracy is neutralized.

## John (Reinier) of Montferrat

- Husband of Maria Komnene Porphyrogenita; initially implicated/punished in the early crisis.
- After service and loyalty, restored as **Caesar** following the Norman victory.
- Alexios publicly seeks patriarchal approval to release Maria (nun-name Irene) from enforced monastic status, signaling political rehabilitation.
- Serves as a trusted high-status bridge to western rulers during crusade diplomacy.

## Bohemond III of Antioch

- Prince of Antioch.
- Roman policy treats prior suzerainty claims as real; after the crusade, arrangements strengthen Roman garrison/succession leverage rather than recognizing full independence.

## Levon of Cilician Armenia

- Receives/holds royal (`rex`) dignity under a settlement that preserves large internal autonomy but acknowledges Roman overlordship.
- Current relation to Constantinople: subordinate ally/client, not independent equal great power.

## Richard I of England

- Joins the crusade after the Roman-German coalition has already changed the campaign's tempo.
- Cooperates pragmatically with Alexios; uses Roman siege/firepower support in Egyptian operations.
- Receives negotiated rewards/relic/commercial benefits but does not acquire Roman gunpowder secrets as a formal transfer.

## Philip II of France

- Crusading western king and dynastic connection through Anna's French origin.
- Relationship with Alexios is useful but transactional; participates in the altered crusade and returns west with religious/political prestige.

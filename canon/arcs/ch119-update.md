# Arc 5 Update — Chapter 119

## Saint Mark Panic: attack → system response

Chapter 119 is the institutional counterstroke to chapter 118.

The narrative emphasis shifts from:
- Dandolo's exploitation of Roman financial fragility

to:
- whether Alexios's accumulated reforms can process a crisis faster than panic destroys confidence.

## Thematic payoff

The state does **not** respond as an omniscient machine from the first second.
- Bankers panic.
- Finance officials panic.
- No one besides Alexios has lived through a crisis of this scale.

The payoff is that the institutions recover quickly because the empire already possesses:
- standardized accounts,
- transaction records,
- paper administration,
- audit capacity,
- common clearing,
- fast posts,
- representative political bodies.

This advances the long-running theme from **great ruler** toward **durable system**.

## Leon's function in the arc

Leon becomes the proof that:
- systemic function can be saved,
- the private owner need not be.

His temporary loss allocation establishes the core crisis doctrine:
**save payments and viable assets; impose first loss on the owner.**

## Dandolo's tactical result

Dandolo's attack genuinely succeeds in creating a large panic and forcing the Roman government to consume fiscal and administrative capacity.

But by chapter 119:
- Constantinople's bond market has stopped falling,
- core payment functions have been preserved,
- ordinary grammation redemption remains credible,
- the crisis has moved from uncontrolled market panic into a political/fiscal contest.

The outcome is therefore not yet a Roman strategic victory: the next test is whether the representative institutions will authorize the fiscal burden.

## Next immediate arc beat

Chapter 119 ends at **“의회라는 문 앞에서.”**

The next unresolved confrontation is:
- Syllogos: emergency supplementary budget and political allocation of public cost.
- Synkletos: potential legal institutionalization/ratification of emergency bank-resolution powers.
- Opposition question: why should public resources protect a financial system whose private actors profited during the boom?

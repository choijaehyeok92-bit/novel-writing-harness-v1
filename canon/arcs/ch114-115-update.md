# Arc 5 Update — Chapters 114–115

> This file updates the current Arc 5 endpoint beyond `canon/arcs/current-arcs.md`, which currently stops at chapter 113.

## Arc 5 — After Barbarossa / Western Succession Rivalry, Sicilian Stalemate, Venetian Split (ch109–CURRENT)

### Core question — UPDATED
Can the eastern Roman state preserve its strategic autonomy as Henry VI converts dynastic claims into continental power, while Venice decides whether Roman-led prosperity is an opportunity or a trap — and can the limited Two-Romes order survive when Balkan successions become proxy-war terrain?

### New locked beats from chapter 114

- Henry's Sicilian invasion does **not** fail militarily on the mainland; it succeeds quickly and establishes Hohenstaufen control across southern mainland centers.
- The decisive Roman response is asymmetric: pre-positioned fortification at Messina/San Raineri, Sicilian loyalty backed by Roman credibility, and maritime denial rather than a major Roman field army.
- The San Raineri **Devil's Horn** becomes the physical symbol of Alexios's long-horizon preparation.
- Roman naval pressure blocks the historical-style easy use of maritime republic fleets. Venice is checked near the Otranto route; Genoa and Pisa refuse to convert contracted Sicilian support into open war against Rome.
- Henry recognizes that continental success is insufficient without an independent naval capability.
- The immediate political result is a de facto divided Sicilian succession: Hohenstaufen mainland control versus William III/Sibylla on the island under Roman protection.
- Henry accepts the immediate compromise but transforms it into a long-term war aim: build sea power, take Constantinople, break Alexios.
- Alexios openly accepts the challenge. The broader Two-Romes war is now consciously anticipated by both rulers.

### New locked beats from chapter 115

- 1195 gives Alexios a private dynastic stake in the coming conflict: Anna safely bears twins, **Ioannes** and **Maria**, both born in the purple.
- Maternal-health reform is now shown as an institutional state program rather than isolated future knowledge: certified midwives, hygiene rules, risk registers, hospital referral, postpartum hemorrhage response and defined extreme obstructed-labor procedure.
- Venice is shown to be **prospering because of Roman trade**, which makes the anti-Roman turn politically complex rather than inevitable.
- Pietro Ziani leads the on-page case for de-escalation and continued access to Roman markets; Domenico Sanudo, Pietro Michiel and Ottaviano Quirino support that side.
- Enrico Dandolo articulates the opposing doctrine: Venice must act before current prosperity becomes irreversible dependence on Roman money, standards, markets and naval order.
- The emerging Roman gold-convertible **grammation** and Roman-required shipping standards become strategic symbols in Dandolo's argument.
- The chapter explicitly carries the Otranto confrontation into Venetian domestic politics and ends the debate with Venice described as politically split.
- 1196 opens a new proxy-war phase: Béla III dies in Hungary and Stefan Nemanja abdicates in Serbia. The narrator locks both successions as future arenas of eastern-western Roman competition, while precise alignments and outcomes remain unstated.

### Current character positions — UPDATED

- **Alexios II:** has successfully converted long-term preparation into a strategic Sicilian denial victory without needing to defeat Henry's army in a major land battle. His family has expanded with Ioannes and Maria, strengthening his personal stake in institutional continuity and security. He now faces a widening contest across finance, maritime power, Venice and the Balkans.
- **Henry VI:** controls the southern mainland but has been denied the island. He accepts the temporary limit only because naval insufficiency makes further advance irrational. His strategic objective now explicitly includes acquiring sea power and eventually attacking Constantinople.
- **Enrico Dandolo:** has moved from covert/strategic counterplay into an explicit domestic doctrine of preventive resistance against Roman dependence. He is not yet politically unchallenged inside Venice.
- **Pietro Ziani:** emerges as the principal on-page Venetian counterweight to Dandolo, defending commercial access and warning that war could hand Roman markets to Genoa and Pisa.
- **William III / Sibylla:** survive on the Sicilian island under Roman-backed maritime deterrence after losing the mainland.
- **Anna:** survives the 1195 twin birth and remains central to Alexios's dynastic/emotional motivation.
- **Manuel:** now has two younger siblings, Ioannes and Maria.

### Active future pressures — UPDATED

- Whether Henry can build or secure an independent fleet large enough to overturn the Messina settlement.
- Whether Constance's planned coronation occurs and how competing Sicilian claims are formalized.
- Whether the Venetian political split can be shifted decisively by conflict in the Adriatic/Dalmatia.
- How the grammation and Roman commercial standards spread and whether Venice, Genoa or Pisa attempt counter-institutions.
- The coming Hungarian succession struggle after Béla III's death.
- The coming Serbian succession struggle after Nemanja's abdication.
- How and when those Balkan conflicts become an explicit eastern-Roman versus western-Roman proxy war.
- Whether papal fear of Hohenstaufen continental expansion pushes Rome toward closer cooperation with Alexios.
- Whether Dandolo can convert strategic anxiety into sufficient Venetian political support for direct war.

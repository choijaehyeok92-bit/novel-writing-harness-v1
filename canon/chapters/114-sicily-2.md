# Chapter 114 Canon — 시칠리아 (2)

> Source: author-approved reviewed manuscript `manuscripts/reviewed/114.시칠리아 (2).txt`. Only on-page outcomes are locked below; inferred later consequences remain open unless separately established.

## Hohenstaufen mainland conquest — ESTABLISHED

- Henry VI enters the Kingdom of Sicily with a **Holy Roman imperial force of about 10,000** and rapidly breaks the mainland regime after Tancred's death.
- Resistance in the mainland is minimal or fragmented. The chapter explicitly frames Tancred as capable but structurally weak in legitimacy, while William III is too young to command equivalent authority.
- Gaeta, Naples, Salerno and Apulia are described as falling in succession without requiring major use of Henry's arquebuses or siege guns.
- The papacy does not actively stop Henry. A tacit accommodation is described in which papal territory is not violated while Henry receives passage/recognition benefits; the pope accepts this largely because he lacks the practical military power to block the imperial army.

## Messina / San Raineri denial complex — ESTABLISHED ALT-HISTORY FORTIFICATION

- The decisive obstacle is not a field defeat but a prepared **Messina Strait denial system** centered on the San Raineri peninsula.
- The fortress, nicknamed the **`Devil's Horn / 악마의 뿔`**, is built around the existing Santa Anna tower and includes low thick walls, several angular projecting artillery positions, a moat, flanking works, deeply protected powder magazines, fixed Pyrobolon positions, low firing positions for Cheirobolon gunners, harbor chains, timber obstacles and floating obstructions.
- Storage is sufficient for roughly **1,000–1,500 defenders** to sustain a prolonged defense.
- The fortress is not claimed to control the entire strait by artillery. Its strategic function is to deny or make prohibitively costly the stable unloading of large numbers of horses, heavy cavalry, siege equipment and supplies.
- Sicilian and Roman double-headed-eagle flags fly together over the position, making Roman involvement overt at the point of crisis.
- Tancred had concentrated fiscal room created by reduced Roman reparations and covert Roman support into Messina so that the island could remain defensible after his death.

## Maritime balance — ESTABLISHED

- Henry attempts to summon Venetian, Genoese and Pisan naval assistance for the fortress problem.
- The **Venetian fleet is checked by the Roman fleet on the route toward Otranto** and cannot simply descend to Messina without risking direct Roman naval battle.
- **Genoa and Pisa turn back**, arguing that war with Rome is outside their contracted obligation; a Sicilian fleet sailing from Palermo also moves to intercept them.
- Henry correctly infers that Alexios has reminded Genoa and Pisa of their Roman commercial privileges and of the danger that an overwhelmingly successful western empire could later reduce Italian communal autonomy.
- This maritime outcome confirms that Henry can dominate the mainland without yet possessing the independent naval lift required to conquer the island.

## Sicilian island survives — CURRENT OUTCOME

- Henry judges direct landing at Messina suicidal and rejects northern or southern bypass landings as operationally unsustainable while Roman and Sicilian fleets remain active.
- The chapter therefore establishes that **Henry cannot conquer the Sicilian island at this stage** without first developing or securing a fleet capable of contesting Roman-Sicilian sea control.
- The Sicilian navy remains aligned with William III's regime in this confrontation because Roman backing makes continued resistance viable.

## Treaty dispute and de facto settlement

- A Roman envoy asserts that Rome still possesses rights in Sicily deriving from the postwar settlement previously accepted by William and later Tancred.
- Henry rejects the claim that he must automatically inherit all restrictions attached to the Tancred regime and considers Roman intervention a breach of the Two-Romes understanding.
- The envoy first offers Roman withdrawal if Henry recognizes Roman treaty rights and guarantees the safety of **William III and Sibylla**; Henry rejects the implications of that formula.
- The underlying Roman proposal is then made explicit: Henry may stop at the mainland and still arrange a lawful Sicilian royal coronation for Constance, while the island itself remains outside his immediate control.
- Henry **accepts stopping at the strait for the present** and orders preparations for Constance's coronation. The chapter does **not** yet depict that coronation itself.
- The resulting political geography is therefore an unresolved dual claim: Hohenstaufen control of the southern mainland versus the surviving William III/Sibylla island regime under Roman naval protection.

## Two-Romes rupture

- Henry treats the forced compromise not as reconciliation but as a strategic declaration of future conflict.
- He vows that once he possesses a fleet capable of ranging the sea, he will take Constantinople and force the Basileus to kneel.
- Alexios's answer is equally explicit: **`오라, 임페라토르.`** and **`짐은 여전히 굶주렸다.`**
- Chapter 114 converts the Sicilian crisis from a limited succession dispute into an openly personal and geopolitical prelude to the coming Two-Romes war.

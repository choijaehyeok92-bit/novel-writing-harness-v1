# Chapter 123 Canon — 무엇을 남길 것인가 (2)

> Source: author-approved manuscript `manuscripts/reviewed/123.무엇을 남길 것인가 (2).txt`.
> Only facts established on-page are locked. Later implementation, enforcement and long-term consequences remain open unless stated below.

## Anti-Capetian encirclement before Bouvines

- Richard I's war against Philip II is framed as strategically favorable to Richard from the outset rather than as a sudden 1199 reversal.
- At Andeli, Richard and **Baldwin IX of Flanders** pledge joint resistance to Philip.
- Richard is stated to have used:
  - a trade embargo as coercive pressure,
  - economic inducements as positive leverage,
  to draw Baldwin into the anti-Capetian alignment.
- **Renaud de Dammartin of Boulogne** joins Richard.
- Brabant, Perche and Blois are also described as aligned with Richard.
- Baldwin attacks Artois and takes Aire and Saint-Omer.

## Gisors — September 1198

- Philip suffers a much heavier defeat at Gisors than in the historical baseline.
- Both sides possess gunpowder weapons by this stage.
- The western empire is producing gunpowder weapons, and military support reaches France.
- The decisive difference is described not as exclusive possession of gunpowder but as **operational experience and doctrine**.
- Richard's advantage derives from:
  - firsthand observation of Roman gunpowder operations at Damietta,
  - post-crusade imitation and improvement through royal workshops,
  - his own military judgment about how to integrate the weapons.

## Richard's early field artillery — ESTABLISHED ALT-HISTORY DEVELOPMENT

- Richard identifies a key operational problem: a gun capable of destroying a fortress is not useful if it cannot reach the chosen battlefield in time.
- His royal workshops develop a lighter field-oriented cannon variant:
  - shortened barrel,
  - reduced caliber,
  - reduced mass relative to the siege pieces,
  - transport on reinforced carts,
  - wheels fixed with wedges for firing,
  - wooden supports used when emplaced.
- These guns are **not** freely maneuvered during battle.
- They can be moved with the army and used from a preselected position to fire limited salvos against dense formations.
- Richard uses such artillery in an **ambush bombardment** at Gisors.
- The narrative describes this as a limited but exceptionally early western European field-artillery capability.

## Consequences of the 1198 victory

- Philip narrowly escapes capture after a bridge collapses during retreat.
- Richard uses the line **“신과 나의 권리다!”**, framed as the phrase that will become the English royal motto.
- Philip's defeat changes the calculations of previously undecided lords:
  - some move toward Richard,
  - others avoid further hostility.
- The viscount of Limousin chooses accommodation rather than rebellion.
- The manuscript explicitly connects this to three factors:
  - Richard has not imposed the historical ransom-related and reconquest-related fiscal pressure on the region;
  - Philip's defeats make outside support for rebellion less credible;
  - Richard's mobile gunpowder capability makes fortified resistance less attractive.
- Consequently the historical chain leading Richard to his death at Châlus does not occur.

## Papal mediation

- Innocent III does not simply ignore the Anglo-French war.
- Papal mediation remains part of the pope's role in Christian politics.
- However, the incentive is weaker than in the historical baseline because:
  - Jerusalem has already been recovered,
  - the crusading objective driving urgent Anglo-French reconciliation is absent,
  - papal attention is heavily occupied by southern Italy and the wider Two-Romes conflict.
- Philip seeks a negotiated draw, but Richard rejects it.

## Philip's northern offensive

- Philip concludes that continued pressure from Normandy in the west and Flanders/Artois in the north is unsustainable.
- He chooses to seek a breakthrough against Flanders.
- His operational goal is to cut the Tournai–Lille–Artois connection.
- Baldwin continues pressure on Artois while Renaud of Boulogne is active.
- Philip is explicitly not portrayed as militarily foolish; the decision is presented as rational under the circumstances.

## Richard's operational deception and maneuver

- Richard maintains the appearance of continuing pressure from Normandy.
- Meanwhile he rapidly moves his main cavalry and elite troops north.
- He uses guides and relay horses supplied by Baldwin.
- Richard moves through Boulogne and Flemish territory and appears in Philip's eastern/southeastern rear.
- By the time Philip identifies the movement, Richard threatens his route back from the Tournai sector toward France.
- Philip withdraws toward Lille–Artois.
- Richard pursues.

## Battle of Bouvines — 1199

- Philip's rear is caught while crossing the Marque at Bouvines.
- The manuscript contrasts the battle with historical 1214 Bouvines:
  - the historical anti-Philip coalition is described as only loosely integrated despite Otto IV's nominal central role;
  - the 1199 coalition has Richard as an effective supreme commander.
- Richard's battle disposition:
  - left: Flemish forces under Baldwin IX,
  - right: Boulogne forces,
  - center: Richard and his knights,
  - mercenaries cooperate with Renaud of Boulogne to attack the rear and cut escape.
- Some French troops cross the bridge, but Philip, the royal knights and the rear remain on the eastern bank/side.
- Baldwin's Flemings hold the French rear in contact.
- Richard sends elite cavalry around to seize the western exit of the Bouvines bridge.
- The Marque and surrounding wet ground restrict escape.
- Philip attempts a breakout personally with the royal knights.
- A stray gunshot kills or disables Philip's horse, causing him to fall.
- His knights surround and defend him, but the escape route is already closed.
- Richard's troops recognize Philip and explicitly order that he be captured rather than killed.
- Philip orders those around him to lay down their arms and personally surrenders to Richard.
- **LOCKED RESULT:** Richard defeats and captures Philip II at Bouvines in 1199.

## Settlement imposed on Philip II

The settlement is heavily dictated by Richard but stops short of destroying the Capetian monarchy.

### Territorial/feudal settlement

1. Philip and his successors permanently renounce French royal:
   - overlordship,
   - homage claims,
   - summons,
   - appellate jurisdiction,
   - confiscation/forfeiture claims,
   over:
   - Normandy,
   - Anjou,
   - Maine,
   - Touraine,
   - Poitou,
   - Aquitaine,
   - Gascony.
2. Philip and crown prince Louis recognize **Edward**, Richard's son, as the lawful heir to Richard's lands on both sides of the Channel.
3. Gisors and principal Vexin castles are handed to Richard; the frontier and castle ownership are to be individually fixed.

### Protection of Richard's coalition and internal order

4. Philip agrees not to retaliate against Flanders, Boulogne and other lords who supported Richard, and to recognize agreed rights/lands and prisoner releases.
5. Philip may not support rebels in England or Richard's continental lands, nor shelter/protect them through the French royal court.

### Secret anti-coalition clause

- A secret clause bars Philip for at least **15 years** from participation in Henry VI's military alignment against:
  - England/the Angevin polity,
  - the Eastern Roman Empire.

### Ransom, guarantees and peace

6. Philip owes **40,000 marks**, divided between immediate payment and installments.
7. Before Philip is released:
   - key castles must be transferred,
   - the first payment must be made;
   after release, hostages from major French princely houses and joint guarantees secure performance.
8. A papal legate and senior clergy of both sides witness the agreement; crown prince Louis and major French princes jointly swear to it.
9. The two sides guarantee at least **15 years of peace**, explicitly tied to the period needed for Edward to grow toward adulthood.
- Philip seals the treaty.

## Constitutional/geopolitical result

- From Normandy through Aquitaine, the Angevin house's continental lands **effectively leave the French crown's overlordship**.
- The chapter does not establish their absorption into a unitary English administrative state.
- Treat the outcome as a cross-Channel Angevin dynastic polity whose continental components retain whatever local structures later canon assigns to them.
- A formal imperial title for Richard is still **not established** unless a later chapter explicitly creates one.

## Henry VI — immediate conclusion

- Henry interprets the transformed western balance as reason to proceed with an attack on the Eastern Roman Empire.
- The chapter characterizes this as a **강행 돌파**.
- The detailed diplomatic steps, any Richard–Henry negotiation, and the final timing/shape of that eastern attack remain open for later canon.

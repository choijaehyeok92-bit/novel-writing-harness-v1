## Henry VI and Richard I — secret agreement concluded

Source: manuscript lines 5–61.

- Henry sees Richard's support for the Welf family and other anti-Henry interests as a danger to his ability to concentrate on the east.
- Conrad of Querfurt proposes acknowledging Richard as king of England, duke of Normandy and Aquitaine, count of Anjou and Maine, and free hereditary ruler of his other lands; Henry is to remain neutral in Richard's later disputes with the French king.
- Henry authorizes the proposal and sends an emissary to England.
- Richard prioritizes stabilizing the inheritance he will leave Edward rather than continuing confrontation with the western empire.
- Richard requires recognition of Edward as lawful heir to England and all Richard's continental lands and a promise not to support lords contesting Edward's succession.
- The imperial emissary asks, in return, for reciprocal non-provision of troops and war funds to each other's enemies.
- Richard accepts and states: `잉글랜드는 동쪽에서 무슨 일이 벌어지든 개입하지 않을 것이오.`
- The narration establishes a **secret pact**, comprising implicit mutual recognition, nonaggression and non-intervention.
- It is explicitly **not a full military alliance**.
- Richard intends to maintain exchange and friendship with Eastern Rome while no longer militarily threatening Henry's rear or intervening in the Two-Romes war.
- Henry's exposure to Richard-backed pressure through Sicily or hostile princes is reduced; this does not establish the end of those actors' independent opposition.
- No duration, treaty name, public charter, exchanged hostages, commercial privileges or enforcement machinery is specified. Do not import the earlier proposed 10–15-year Richard–Henry arrangements; chapter 123's 15-year French obligations are a different agreement.

## Anatolian proxy front — rebellion ends

Source: manuscript lines 76–92; supersedes chapter 117's ongoing-stalemate status for this rebellion.

- **쿠트브 앗딘 / Qutb al-Din Malikshah**, the claimant opposed to Kaykhusraw, is killed in his sleep by the emirs who had supported him.
- His severed head is presented to **케이휘스레브 / Kaykhusraw** as an offering of surrender.
- The emirs change sides because Caesarea remains untaken, winter is approaching and Danishmendid attacks keep troubling their rear.
- They calculate that Kaykhusraw's dependence on Roman support and limited authority will make it difficult for him to purge them.
- The chapter states that this Seljuk-front episode has ended. It does not establish a comprehensive new internal settlement, a purge, or the elimination of every future Anatolian threat.
- Kaykhusraw receives his elder brother's head with mixed bitterness and relief.

## Cilician Armenia — Levon remains contained

Source: manuscript lines 94–107.

- The Hetumids pursue an opportunity to seize power and, also acting under Roman instigation, keep Levon's forces tied down.
- Levon cannot exert effective pressure on Rome from the east.
- German/Venetian support is harder to deliver here than in Hungary and Serbia.
- No fall of Levon, capture of his capital, completed Hetumid takeover or formal peace is established.
- Reduced eastern pressure allows Rome to concentrate attention on Hungary and Serbia. No exact troop transfer is stated.

## Hungary and Serbia — western-backed factions deteriorate

Source: manuscript lines 65–74, 109–118.

- Vukan reports that he cannot hold out even another six months. This is an in-story warning, not a precise dated forecast.
- Andrew and Vukan continue to need resources while failing to advance against the Roman-supported opposition.
- The military/political balance gradually moves against the western empire and Venice.
- Emeric is framed as the legitimate Hungarian king; even lords who supported Andrew increasingly hedge their support.
- Stefan's side retains Roman military support, with a Roman tagma explicitly present in Serbia.
- Neither Andrew nor Vukan is yet killed, captured or conclusively defeated. No revised map or newly opened invasion corridor is supplied.

## Venice — coalition finance and regime fragility

Source: manuscript lines 120–138.

- A substantial portion of the funds and goods reaching Andrew and Vukan is provided through **pro-Dandolo merchant houses**.
- Dandolo has attracted their money by promising future commercial privileges and war revenues, but returns have not appeared and tied-up funds are increasing.
- Merchants openly press him for the promised western commercial access and eastern campaign, threatening his political survival.
- Dandolo's dictatorship remains fragile; its fall and a successor regime moving toward Rome are anticipated risks, not events that have occurred.
- Do not expand this into a claim that Venice finances a specified share of all imperial warfare or that every Venetian merchant is insolvent.

## Imperial-princely compact — concessions exchanged for expedition support

Source: manuscript lines 146–181.

- Conrad distinguishes uncompromising opponents from princes principally seeking security for their lands and status.
- The compact requires lawful hearings and judgments with princely participation before confiscation of fiefs, removal of ducal rank or denial of succession rights, rather than action on an imperial official's report alone.
- **Retained source exception:** the emperor may intervene urgently in cases of manifest armed rebellion, established infringement of rights, or absence of a lawful heir. The manuscript does not specify the exact procedure or outer limits of that intervention. Preserve this wording; do not replace it with an earlier editorial suggestion.
- Recovery of property disputed between the imperial household and princes is temporarily suspended until judgment, rather than permanently renounced.
- Princes' lawful rights are reconfirmed. New royal castles, markets and towns require consultation or dispute review.
- Unilateral royal acquisition of a deceased bishop's property is restricted, exploitation of vacant diocesan revenues is restrained, and lawful elections and existing diocesan rights are respected.
- In return, Henry obtains agreed troops and transport plus promises that princes will not invade one another's lands or rebel during the expedition.
- The narration identifies substantial retreat from earlier reforms. It does not abolish imperial authority, create fully independent principalities or specify a new permanent imperial parliament/regency institution.

## Frederick VI — released and rehabilitated

Source: manuscript lines 159–165; supersedes his earlier confinement status.

- Henry confirms that Frederick was not guilty of treason.
- Frederick is released and recovers his ducal honor, property management and legitimate rights of audience/contact as Duke of Swabia.
- Henry requests his participation in the forthcoming expedition as a senior commander.
- Frederick accepts his duty as a subject and Hohenstaufen family member; his personal loyalty is preserved.
- This is not a pardon of an established crime. No independent regency, succession promotion or exact field command is conferred on-page.

## Henry's wager

Source: manuscript lines 140–195, 228–248.

- Henry sees direct victory over Eastern Rome as the means to avoid domestic loss of authority and recover the political ground conceded to princes.
- His internal formula is `따서 갚으면 된다.`
- He seeks the prestige of an emperor of both Romes, exceeding Barbarossa and enabling renewed reform; this is an ambition, not an attained title or outcome.
- He chooses action without sending an envoy for an ultimatum and distinguishes anticipating invasion from knowing when forces will move.
- Justifications: Sicilian territorial demands claimed through Constance, and requests for assistance from Andrew and Vukan.
- He draws his sword and orders the ministeriales to advance. Exact departure date, route, strength, border crossing and first engagement are not shown.

## Alexios — anticipated withdrawal, staff summons and confidence

Source: manuscript lines 199–224, 250–258.

- Ana calls Richard's action betrayal; Alexios says he expected it.
- Alexios wanted Richard to engage Philip and impose western pressure on Henry. Additional domestic political costs for Henry were a bonus.
- Richard has exceeded that minimum by destroying one axis of the opposing coalition; Alexios does not demand unlimited further loyalty.
- Skepsis reporting, the multi-front operations and Venice's coup make an eventual direct imperial attack foreseeable. Richard's pressure bought preparation time.
- Alexios summons **세크레톤 투 스트라토페두 / Sekreton tou Stratopedou**, continuing the field-staff institution established in chapter 113, not introducing a differently named modern General Staff.
- He explicitly trusts the empire he has built. No detailed Roman operational plan or successful entrapment of Henry is yet disclosed.

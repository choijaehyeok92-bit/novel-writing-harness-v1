# Chapter 118 Canon — 성 마르코 공황

> Source: author-approved reviewed manuscript `manuscripts/reviewed/118.성 마르코 공황.txt`.
> Only facts established on-page are locked below. Proposed countermeasures discussed outside the manuscript are not treated as enacted canon.

## Date and event

- **1198-04-25, Feast of Saint Mark**: the financial crisis later called the **성 마르코의 공황 / Panic of Saint Mark** begins.
- The chapter explicitly frames the crisis as sudden on the surface but structurally prepared by the expansion of partial-reserve-style banking and aggressive credit creation inside the Roman financial system.
- Venetian action is an intentional trigger, but the manuscript rejects the idea that Venice alone created the underlying fragility.

## Leon Chrysocheir and the Chrysocheir trapeza

- **Leon Chrysocheir** is one of the empire's Megas Trapezitai and has the largest pool of funds under management among them.
- He is known as the **“황금 손”**.
- His business model combines:
  - deposits,
  - trade finance,
  - government-bond dealing,
  - acceptance/guarantee of commercial bills,
  - loans to merchants,
  - investment in ships, warehouses and trade ventures,
  - holdings of `황은채`.
- His key operating principle is to avoid leaving deposited gold and silver idle; he transforms short-call deposits into longer-dated assets.
- He is highly competent rather than foolish, but his competence and confidence push the business beyond a safe liquidity margin.
- He has guaranteed transactions worth several times his own capital/funds.
- Theodore Argyros explicitly warns Leon that the business is becoming too risky, especially Leon's willingness to guarantee other people's bills.
- Leon assumes that his importance to the imperial payment network and the existence of **Koini Trapeza** mean the system cannot allow his institution to disappear.

## Venetian opening attack

- On Saint Mark's feast, Venetian merchants across the empire withdraw their deposits from Chrysocheir branches in large numbers.
- Leon orders payment rather than suspension because he understands that refusal itself would destroy confidence.
- Venetian holders then begin heavy sales of `황은채`.
- Leon identifies the sales as an artificial/coordinated attack and initially orders his trapeza to buy the bonds in order to defend confidence and the value of its own holdings.
- By this point Leon states that roughly half his reserve remains.

## Panic transmission

- Rumors spread through the `Kitrina Fyla`, including claims that the imperial treasury is empty.
- Roman depositors who were not part of the Venetian operation begin withdrawing funds defensively.
- Venetian merchants then demand immediate settlement of maturing bills and refuse ordinary extensions.
- Many such bills bear Leon Chrysocheir's guarantee, causing contingent liabilities to become immediate payment obligations against his trapeza.

## Bond crash and liquidity crisis

- Facing insufficient reserves, Leon reverses from major buyer to forced seller of `황은채` in order to obtain cash.
- This reversal becomes a major accelerant of the bond-market collapse.
- Within several days, `황은채` prices fall below **60% of face value**.
- Market confidence is further destroyed by the simple public signal that “Chrysocheir is selling the bonds.”
- Falling bond prices damage collateral values.
- Chrysocheir's staff demands additional collateral from borrowers, but borrowers themselves cannot obtain liquidity from trapezai.
- Leon's institution remains, at this stage, **asset-positive on its books**: receivables still exceed payables.
- The immediate failure is therefore a **liquidity crisis**: it lacks gold, silver and grammation needed for immediate payment.

## Payment suspension

- Depositors crowd Chrysocheir's institution demanding their money.
- Leon finally declares **지급정지**, described narratively as a de facto default.
- Leon's reasoning is that Chrysocheir has become systemically central:
  - market-making in `황은채`,
  - commercial-bill settlement,
  - maritime lending,
  - a major part of the commercial payment network.
- He believes Alexios and Koini Trapeza must save him because allowing Chrysocheir to fail would damage the wider payment system.

## Dandolo's strategy and interpretation

- Enrico Dandolo deliberately exploits this moral-hazard dynamic.
- Dandolo regards Koini Trapeza as an intelligent and impressive institution but recognizes that its existence can encourage private trapezai to assume greater risks if they expect rescue.
- Venice itself is attempting to imitate Koini Trapeza by creating a central banking institution, but factional and family interests are obstructing the project.
- Dandolo states that he did not want to use this method because the attack will make it obvious to Alexios that Venice is responsible and may destroy remaining Venetian privileges.
- He nevertheless proceeds because:
  - the proxy fronts have failed to impose enough cost on Rome,
  - supporting those fronts is becoming increasingly expensive for Venice and Henry VI,
  - the Angevin–French war is consuming western imperial resources,
  - papal politics are unfavorable,
  - both Henry VI and Dandolo face internal political constraints.
- Dandolo's immediate objective is to create enough financial pressure to force the Roman government toward negotiation.

## Papal / Hungarian political fact

- The manuscript establishes **Innocent III as newly elected in January 1198**.
- In this timeline he openly supports Emeric's legitimate Hungarian kingship and **excommunicates Andrew**.
- This is a locked alternate-history development in the manuscript and should not be silently replaced by the historical baseline.

## Chapter endpoint

- Dandolo challenges Alexios to respond.
- Alexios answers:
  - **“이럴 때를 위해 코이니 트라페자와 싱클레토스, 실로고스를 만들었다.”**
- Chapter 118 therefore ends **before** the detailed imperial countermeasure is enacted on-page.
- Do not treat any proposed emergency lending, supplementary budget, bank-resolution law, deposit guarantee or Synkletos/Syllogos procedure as canon until a later manuscript establishes it.

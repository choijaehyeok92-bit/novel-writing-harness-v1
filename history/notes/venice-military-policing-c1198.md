# Historical Research Note — Venice military and public-order structure c. 1190s

> Layer: `history/`, not story canon.
> Scope: historical baseline for reviewing Venice scenes around 1198.
> Rule: where story canon intentionally diverges (for example the treaty-established Constantinople bailo), canon prevails.

## High-confidence baseline

### Civic military obligation and fleet-centered mobilization — Evidence B

Aldo A. Settia's *Storia di Venezia* chapter on the military apparatus describes Venetian military organization in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries as fundamentally amphibious. The civic militia was closely tied to war-fleet mobilization rather than divided cleanly into a land army and navy.

Relevant points:
- military obligation applied broadly to men under Venetian jurisdiction;
- a 1188 call is described as reaching Venetian men across status groups and requiring their return for mobilization;
- subject/allied Adriatic communities could be required to furnish ships or contingents;
- Venice could mobilize substantial manpower without maintaining a modern permanent national army.

**Review implication:** do not assume a large modern standing army in 1198 Venice.

## Mercenaries — Evidence C

The same specialist survey records twelfth-century traditions/precedents for hiring foreign troops and notes foreign mercenary use as a developing Venetian practice, while also warning that documentation for the period is incomplete.

**Review implication:**
- a small, deniable body of foreign hired men is plausible;
- the exact existence, size, employer and permanence of a specific 1198 German mercenary unit must come from story canon, not be asserted from history alone.

## Public order and night watch — Evidence B/C

A *Storia di Venezia* chapter on public finance notes that around 1200 Venice had roughly sixty `contrade`, which bore public burdens including **night-watch obligations**.

The military survey also stresses that for the twelfth–thirteenth centuries it can be difficult to separate military organization from organs for public order and armed coastal/river watch, and that documentation is limited.

**Review implication:**
- do not model 1198 Venice as having a modern centralized professional police service;
- local/community night-watch obligations are appropriate background;
- a noisy armed seizure of major nobles should eventually create local political/public-order consequences unless the plot supplies concealment, speed or official cover.

## Minor Council and ducal constraint — Evidence B

Gerhard Rösch / institutional scholarship summarized in *Il primo comune* establishes:
- a Minor Council is documented by 1187;
- six councillors are documented in 1189;
- Dandolo's 1192 `promissio` shows the doge operating under strong council constraints;
- the study concludes that without unanimous consent of his councillors the doge could in practice do very little in the relevant sphere.

**Review implication:**
- chapter 121's six-member Minor Council is historically supportable;
- a coordinated council defection can plausibly paralyze ducal action politically;
- this is not the same as a modern statutory impeachment procedure.

## Later institutions — guardrail

Do **not** automatically back-project later, more specialized Venetian policing/security magistracies into 1198.
If a later office such as a mature `Signori di Notte` structure is proposed, require period-specific sourcing or treat it as an explicit alternate-history divergence.

## Specific inference for chapter 121 — Evidence D

Given the historical mix of civic military obligation, incomplete professional public-order structures, and attested/possible mercenary use, the most conservative reading of the chapter-121 German-accented attackers is:

- off-book hired armed men,
- potentially supported by private/party networks,
- **not automatically official Venetian troops or police**.

This is a plausibility inference, not a direct historical attestation of Dandolo possessing such a unit.
